How to prepare for a recording session

Studio time is paid by the hour, so the work you do before you walk in decides how much you get out of it. Use this checklist before your next session.

How a session works

  1. Setup. Your engineer loads your beat or session and sets the mic and headphone mix. Tell them up front what you want to get done, so they can plan the time.
  2. Warm-up and test takes. A few passes to set levels and get comfortable in the booth.
  3. Main takes. Usually several full takes of each section, then punch-ins to fix single lines.
  4. Doubles, ad-libs and harmonies. The layers that make a vocal sound finished.
  5. Rough mix and files. You leave with a rough bounce to live with before the proper mix.

First time in a studio? Book a free 30-minute studio visit first, or a Crash Test for an honest evaluation of where your sound is and what to work on.

Before the day

  • Finish the writing. Write and memorize the lyrics before you book. Writing in the booth is the most expensive way to write.
  • Rehearse with the actual beat, out loud, at full volume, standing up the way you'll record.
  • Know your song's details: the BPM, the key if you know it, and where every section starts.
  • Plan the session. One song done properly beats three songs rushed. If you have more, book a longer block. A 2-hour session gives you time to warm up and still finish.
  • Pick a reference song, a released track whose vocal sound you like.

What to bring

  • Your beat as a high-quality WAV (not an MP3 ripped from a video). If you're working with a producer, ask for the tracked-out stems.
  • Your lyrics, on paper or your phone, even if you know them.
  • Water at room temperature.
  • Your own headphones, if you're used to them.
  • A way to receive files: an email address or a cloud drive with free space.

Getting your voice ready

  • Sleep and hydrate the day before. Your voice shows tiredness more than anything else.
  • Warm up for 10 to 15 minutes before you arrive: humming, lip trills and sirens through your range.
  • Avoid shouting the day before, and go easy on anything that dries you out or makes you clear your throat a lot. Many singers skip dairy, alcohol and very cold drinks before a session.
  • Pace yourself. Record the hardest, highest parts while your voice is fresh, and save ad-libs for later.

In the booth

  • Keep a steady distance from the mic. Your engineer will show you where to stand.
  • Ask for the headphone mix you need: more vocal, less beat, or a little reverb to perform with.
  • Don't chase perfection on the first take. Get a full pass, then fix lines.
  • Listen back at the end before you leave, so nothing is missing when you get to the mix.

After the session

Live with the rough mix for a day or two and write down what you'd change. Then book mixing and mastering; our guide to mixing vs mastering explains the difference. Not sure your demo is ready? Get free AI demo feedback first.
